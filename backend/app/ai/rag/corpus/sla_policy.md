# Citywide SLA Policy

## Priority bands

Every complaint receives a priority score from 0 to 100, produced by scoring
four factors — category severity, population impact, safety risk, and
urgency — each on a 0-25 scale and summing them. The score maps onto one of four risk bands: 0–25 is low, 26–50 is medium, 51–75 is high, and 76–100 is critical.
A complaint's response window is set by its band, not by its category, so a
low-severity electrical fault and a low-severity pothole carry the same
window.

## Response windows

Critical complaints are responded to within 4 hours of intake.
High complaints are responded to within 24 hours of intake.
Medium complaints are responded to within 72 hours of intake.
Low complaints are responded to within 168 hours of intake.
"Response" means a work order is opened and a first inspection or action is
logged against it — it does not require full resolution within the window,
only that the assigned team has engaged with the case.

## Warning thresholds

Two thresholds trigger automatic warnings ahead of a breach. At 50% of the
response window elapsed with no response logged, the system flags the case
and notifies the assigned ward or zonal officer. At 75% of the window
elapsed, the case is flagged as at risk of SLA breach and the notification
escalates one level up the ladder below, so a supervisor is aware before the
deadline is missed rather than after.

## Escalation ladder

Every department follows the same four-level ladder: ward, then block, then
district, then city. A case starts with the ward-level responder named in
its department's SOP. If unresolved past the 50% warning threshold, it moves
to the block supervisor covering that ward cluster. Past the 75% threshold,
it moves to the district engineer or officer, who holds authority to
reassign resources from elsewhere in the district. A full breach of the
window, or a case that meets a department-specific immediate-escalation
condition (for example, a live wire, a confirmed disease cluster, or a
structure at collapse risk), escalates directly to the city-level department
head regardless of how much of the window remains, since those conditions
represent an ongoing risk to life or citywide infrastructure rather than a
scheduling delay.
