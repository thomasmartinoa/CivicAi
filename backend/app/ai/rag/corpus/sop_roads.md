# Public Works Department — Road Surface SOP

## Scope

ROADS covers damage to the surface of an existing, previously serviceable road:
potholes, cracked or sunken pavement, broken or missing dividers, faded lane
markings on a structurally sound carriageway, and damaged footpaths or kerbs
running alongside a road. It also covers debris or fallen material obstructing
a road surface where the road itself is not otherwise being worked on.

Two cases are handed to another category rather than kept here. First, a
trench dug by a utility contractor (water, electricity, telecom) for a cable
or pipe and left unfilled or badly reinstated is CONSTRUCTION, not ROADS —
the road surface is only a casualty of someone else's excavation, and Public
Works cannot dispatch a resurfacing crew to a site that is still an open dig.
Second, standing rainwater pooling on a road because a storm drain is blocked
is FLOODING, not ROADS, even when the citizen describes it as "the road is
damaged" — the surface underneath is frequently intact and the fix is drain
clearance, not asphalt work. A ward engineer confirms which applies during
intake when the complaint text is ambiguous.

## Ownership

Public Works Department owns this category citywide. The head officer for
Public Works is the escalation contact of first resort; each ward additionally
has an assistant engineer who takes first-line responsibility for potholes and
footpath damage inside that ward before a case reaches the head office.

## Response norms

Response windows are driven by the complaint's risk band, not the category:
critical within 4 hours, high within 24 hours, medium within 72 hours, and low
within 168 hours. A pothole large enough to unseat a two-wheeler or positioned
on a school route is typically critical or high; a faded lane marking on a
low-traffic service road is typically low. The assistant engineer logs a site
visit before the window elapses even when a permanent fix takes longer —
temporary barricading or cold-mix patching counts as a response.

## Typical materials and cost drivers

Hot-mix asphalt for surface patching, cold-mix asphalt for emergency
temporary repairs, aggregate base material for sub-surface failures, precast
concrete kerb stones, thermoplastic paint for lane markings, and steel or
concrete divider segments. Cost is driven mainly by the area and depth of the
failed surface (a sub-surface failure costs far more than a skin-deep
pothole), by whether traffic control and night work are required on a high
traffic road, and by how many times the same stretch has already been
patched — repeat failures point to a drainage problem underneath that must be
fixed first or the patch fails again within a season.

## Escalation

A pothole or surface defect is first assigned to the ward assistant engineer.
If unresolved past 50% of the response window, it escalates to the block
supervisor covering that ward cluster. Past 75% of the window, or if the
defect is on a designated arterial or bus route, it escalates to the district
engineer. A defect that has caused a reported accident, or one on a road
carrying more than one ward's traffic, escalates directly to the city-level
Public Works head office regardless of elapsed time.
